Giant heat pumps that can warm whole districts

City-scale heat pumps are moving from pilot projects to industrial-scale infrastructure. Here is how they work, why they are getting bigger, and what a flagship project on the River Rhine is aiming to deliver.

1) Why this matters

District heating networks distribute hot water (or steam) through insulated pipes to multiple buildings. Decarbonising these networks is a priority for many cities—especially where a single heat source can influence thousands of homes at once.

The emerging approach: treat low‑grade environmental heat (from air, ground, rivers, wastewater, or industrial waste heat) as a “fuel”, and upgrade it using heat pumps. When connected to a heat network, one plant can support an entire district.

Key idea: a big heat pump is not a bigger domestic unit. It is closer to a power-station-scale refrigeration system, engineered for continuous operation, network temperatures, and grid constraints.

2) How a district-scale heat pump works

The thermodynamic principle is the same as a household heat pump: a refrigerant evaporates at low temperature, then compression boosts its temperature so heat can be delivered at useful levels.

At district scale, the “heat source” can be a river or the sea. Large volumes of water flow through heat exchangers, and the pump harvests a small temperature difference—then returns the water to the source.

Heat source

River / seawater
Large, steady flow keeps performance predictable.

Upgrade step

Compression

Delivery

Hot water piped to many buildings from one plant.

3) Case study: pulling heat from the Rhine

In Mannheim (Germany), energy firm MVV Energie has announced plans for very large heat pump units connected to the local district heating network, located at a site that historically relied on fossil generation.

The concept is straightforward but the civil and mechanical engineering is not: take in river water at high flow rates, extract a modest amount of heat, and discharge the water back with minimal temperature change.

Engineering constraints tend to define the “maximum sensible size”: available electricity supply, network temperature requirements, environmental permits, and what can be built and maintained reliably.

4) The missing piece: storage and flexibility

Big heat pumps often pair with hot-water thermal storage tanks. Storage lets operators smooth peaks, avoid running equipment at maximum output all the time, and take advantage of lower‑carbon or lower‑cost electricity when available.

Operationally, this shifts the question from “Can we meet the peak hour?” to “Can we balance heat demand over the day without overbuilding the plant?” It is also a key enabler when renewable electricity supply varies.

Storage can cover spikes without oversizing compressors.

Grid-aware operation

Resilience

Stored heat provides short-term buffering for outages or maintenance.
